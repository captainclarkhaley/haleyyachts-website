## 1. The call

I am taking the property angle, and I want to sharpen it rather than just agree with it.

The reading is right. A yacht listing dropped into a real estate magazine is an ad in the wrong language. The reader is not shopping a boat, they are shopping a house, and the thing they have just acquired or are about to acquire is a dock. A dock with nothing on it is an unfinished purchase. Clark is who you call about that. The boat is the illustration, the offer is him.

Where I want to push is on the word "him." An ad whose subject is a person is the single most category-typical thing in a real estate magazine. Every other spread in that book is a realtor with a headshot and a tagline, and the reader has trained themselves to skip it. So the offer cannot be Clark's personality. It has to be a specific, concrete, free thing he does that nobody else in the magazine offers: **tell me your address and I will tell you what boat fits your dock.** Length, depth at dead low, bridge clearance to the inlet. Three numbers that rule out more boats than budget ever does, that nobody mentions at the closing table, and that a yacht broker can answer in one phone call. That is the offer. Clark is the person attached to it. The credibility that makes the offer believable is his 50,000 miles, not his face.

That is also why I am pushing back on the half-page portrait. Clark suggested the 545 on one side and a photo of him with some information on the other. I am keeping his structure, the boat on the left and the human on the right, but the portrait sits at about two inches, not half a page. At half a page it reads as a realtor ad. At two inches it reads as an editorial byline, which is the register we want in this book. Clark now has high-resolution photography of himself, so a larger portrait is technically possible. I still would not do it, for the reason above.

**On the boat-show tension.** I am not splitting the difference, and I am not designing for the show floor. Here is why. The magazine at FLIBS is still a real estate magazine, sitting in a rack next to two hundred publications that are all about boats. If we compete on "look at this beautiful yacht," we lose to every builder with a real ad budget and a stand fifty feet away. The one reader who is in both modes at once is the Florida waterfront homeowner walking the show trying to work out what fits behind the house, and that reader is exactly who the dock angle is written for. So the show is not a second audience needing a second message. It is a distribution multiplier for the message we already have.

We harvest the show with one line and a QR code, not with the design. There is a single sentence in the contact area inviting a show reader to text Clark and walk the docks with him. That costs us nothing, converts the reader who is standing in the show right now, and does not contort the spread. If Clark is not attending FLIBS, we cut that line and lose nothing.

**One structural virtue worth naming.** Because the spread leads with the offer and not the boat, the hero boat is a swap and not a rewrite. Every word of the headline, deck, body, bio and contact block is boat-independent. Only two blocks change, and they are marked as swap blocks in section 3. Do not let this get rebuilt as a listing ad, or a sale kills the spread.

## 1b. Which boat, and whether to lead with a boat at all

Numbered 1b so the section numbering in the original brief still lines up.

### Should there be a specific boat on the spread?

Yes, and here is the test that keeps it honest. This is a picture magazine and the reader needs one concrete object to hang the abstraction on. An ad about a service, with no boat in it, is an ad with no picture, and it will die on the page. But the boat has to earn its place by **proving the argument rather than being the thing for sale.** If the headline says the dock decides what fits, the boat on the page has to be a boat that fits. That single test decides the next question.

### 545 versus 4300 SE

| | Riviera 545 SUV, "Fringe Benefits" | Riviera 4300 Sports Express |
|---|---|---|
| Status | Used brokerage listing, Clark's own | New build, arriving July |
| Can it sell out from under the ad? | Yes. The issue has a shelf life of months. | No, not in the same way. A build slot is replaced, not lost. |
| Print-resolution photography | Depends on the photographer who shot that listing. One phone call, unknown answer. | Riviera's dealer and press library, which a dealer is entitled to pull from. Our own listing page already hotlinks from rivieraaustralia.com, so the library is real and reachable. |
| LOA | 55 ft (or 54 ft, our own material disagrees) | 45 ft 6 in |
| Beam | Not on file | 14 ft 7 in |
| Maximum draft | Not on file | **4 ft 2 in** |
| Fits a typical Palm Beach residential dock? | For a good part of the readership, no | Yes, and we can print the numbers |
| Price on the page | $1,295,000, credible and filtering | No public price, we lose that |
| Lead economics for Clark | His own listing, showable today, fifteen minutes away | Longer conversation, further-out close, dealer context |

**My recommendation is the 4300 Sports Express**, and the deciding reason is not shelf life or photography. Both of those points are correct and I agree with them. The deciding reason is that the 4300 is the only one of the two that proves the argument.

The whole spread says three numbers decide what you can keep behind your house: length, depth, clearance. The 4300 is 45 ft 6 in long with a **maximum draft of 4 ft 2 in**. That is a boat that fits a normal residential dock on a Palm Beach canal. I can print that draft figure in the detail panel and let it carry the argument on its own, which is a far better use of the panel than a price. Run the 545 instead and I am telling a reader that the dock decides, while illustrating it with a fifty-five foot boat that a large share of their docks will not take. The ad argues against itself.

Two supporting reasons that also favour the 4300 for this readership specifically:

The 4300 has Volvo Penta IPS with joystick docking. For a homeowner who has never docked a boat and is quietly nervous about putting one behind their own house in front of their neighbours, joystick docking is the most reassuring single fact we could put on the page. That is a property-buyer benefit, not a boat-buyer benefit, and no other yacht ad in that magazine will be speaking to that fear.

The 4300 also sits closer to where a first-boat waterfront owner actually enters the market than a $1.295M fifty-five footer does. Better qualified-lead economics from a property audience.

**The honest cost of choosing it.** Clark gives up a price on the page, which costs us some credibility and some self-filtering, and he gives up promoting a listing that is his alone and showable this afternoon. The price problem is solvable: either replace the asking line with "Build slots available for 2027 delivery" or, better, get a from-price Clark is comfortable printing. See section 6.

**Keep the 545 as the fallback,** in one of two cases: Riviera's press library does not deliver usable print files in time, or Clark decides he wants his own listing carried regardless. Section 3 gives complete, ready-to-set copy for both. Nothing else in the spread changes either way.

**Do not run both boats.** One boat, one argument. Two boats halves the page and turns the spread back into a listing ad.

## 2. Layout

### The idea in one paragraph, for the designer

Left page is a single photograph, edge to edge, nothing else on it but a headline reversed out of the sky and a small caption at the foot. It is deliberately quiet and it does not look like an advertisement. Right page is an editorial page on white: a deck, two columns of body copy, a navy panel carrying the boat's details, a small portrait with a bio, and a contact bar. The two pages are tied together by a single cyan rule bleeding off the bottom of the whole spread. That rule is the only element that crosses the gutter, and it carries no information, so the fold cannot damage it.
